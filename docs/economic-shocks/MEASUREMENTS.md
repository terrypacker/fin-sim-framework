## 1. Equity: depth, grind, and time back to the prior peak

Two sources, deliberately. **Shiller** (`Shiller-SP500-monthly.csv`) is the S&P 500 —
the index the popular "−49 %" figures are quoted from — as a monthly AVERAGE, so it
is shallower than the intraday peak-to-trough numbers in press accounts. The **OECD**
indices (`SPASTT01USM661N` / `SPASTT01AUM661N`) are one methodology applied to both
countries, which is what makes the US-vs-AU asymmetry a like-for-like comparison — and
they are BROAD indices, so the US line is shallower than the S&P in a bust concentrated
in large-cap tech. All are PRICE indices: dividends excluded, which is the right basis
for a level effect (the model reinvests dividends separately).

## 3. Dividends — what `dividendAdjustment` is calibrated against

`dividendAdjustment: -0.40` means *this shock cuts the dividend YIELD by 40 %* — it
multiplies the payout, it is not a percentage-point move. So the number to compare it
to is the peak-to-trough fall in **dividends per share**, not in the yield (which rises
in a crash because the price falls faster than the payout).

estimate of the same quantity.

## 7. FX — USD_AUD drift and volatility

`DEXUSAL` is quoted as **USD per AUD**; the model's `USD_AUD` is **AUD per USD**
(`exchangeRateUsdToAud`, default 1.55), so the model rate is 1/DEXUSAL and the signs
are opposite. Both are shown. `fxAdjustment` is ADDITIVE in model units, so on a 1.55
base an `fxAdjustment: 0.08` is a 5.2 % AUD depreciation.

**The AUD floated on 12 December 1983.** Before that the rate was pegged (to sterling,
then the USD, then a trade-weighted basket), so a pre-1984 USD_AUD move is an
administrative decision, not a market outcome, and the 1970s USD story cannot be read
off it. The trade-weighted-dollar rows in 7b are the series that story actually lives in.
