# Economic shocks — what each one does, and why it is shaped that way

Audience: someone choosing a shock in the workbench, or reading a study that ran one.
It is written to be liftable into a UI help panel more or less as-is.

- **Mechanics** (the reducers, the action types, the framework decisions) live in
  `design/21-financial-shock-and-regime-framework.md`.
- **The presets themselves** live in `src/finance/economic-shocks/shock-library.js`.
- **The evidence** for every number below is in `MEASUREMENTS.md`, generated by
  `scripts/probes/measure-shock-history.mjs` from the sources in `data/`
  (provenance: `SOURCES.md`).

Section references like *(M §1)* point at `MEASUREMENTS.md`.

## 1. What a shock is

A shock is **not** a state you switch on. It is two separate things that happen at one
moment and then decay on a clock:

**A level effect** — an instantaneous revaluation, applied once, at the shock's start
date. `equityRevaluation: { rateKeys: ['EQUITY_US'], multiplier: -0.40 }` multiplies every
equity sleeve tagged `EQUITY_US` by 0.60 on that date. Nothing about it persists; it is a
haircut to the book, and the book carries on from there. Cost basis is untouched, so the
haircut shows up as an unrealized loss, not a realized one — while the *contribution*
ledger (`earningsBasis`) is rescaled with the balance, so a crash cannot leave an account
claiming more basis than it has value (design 84 §G8).

**A regime** — a persistent adjustment to *forward* rates, pushed onto a stack
(`state.activeRegimes`). While it is live, every rate the simulation reads is
`base + Σ(regime adjustment × that regime's recovery factor)`. The recovery factor starts
at 1.0 and decays to 0 along the recovery curve, at which point the regime drops off the
stack and the world is back to baseline.

Three consequences worth internalizing before reading any result:

1. **A preset does not state its drawdown.** It states a level break, a drag, a profile and
   a duration, and the drawdown is what those four *compose* to. `CALIBRATION.md` prints
   what each one actually produces, measured by running the engine.
2. **The level break is only the front-loaded part of the fall.** Measured: 8 % of the GFC's
   fall and 17 % of the dot-com's happened in the first three months, against 82 % of
   COVID's (M §1). So fast episodes are level breaks and slow ones are mostly drag — which
   is why the numbers vary so much from preset to preset.
3. **Equity growth is applied once a year.** Earnings fire on a year-end series, so a
   regime's recovery factor is only *sampled* for equity on 31 December. A decline is
   therefore expressible only in whole years — the GFC preset bottoms at 24 months against
   a measured 17 and cannot do better. Cash and fixed income are applied monthly and do see
   the finer curve.

### Multi-leg shocks

Real episodes run on more than one clock. In 2000–2002 the equity bust ground for
~30 months while the monetary easing that answered it ran for years — the funds rate was
still at 1 % in 2004. A shock can therefore declare `legs`, each with its own `regime` and
its own `recovery`; each becomes an independent regime on the same stack. `DOTCOM_2000_LITE`
and `LOST_DECADE_2000` both use this. Sharing one curve made the bond rally round-trip by
construction — the framework deleting the exact protection the episode is famous for
(design 21 §19).

### Tags, and the stress window

A preset may **tag** itself, and the tags are the only thing the behavioral layer
(`design/29`) gates on. `PANIC_SELL`, `CASH_BUCKET_DRAWDOWN`, `DOWNTURN_ROTH_CONVERSION`,
`CONTRIBUTION_SUSPENSION` and the crash-entry path of `OPPORTUNISTIC_REBALANCE` /
`TARGET_ALLOCATION` all read `regime.tags`; an untagged shock leaves every one of them
configurable and inert.

Tags sit on **one leg**, and that leg's `durationMonths` is the **stress window** — a tag
does not decay with the recovery factor, it is present or absent while its regime lives. So
each tagged preset carries a dedicated `stress` leg with no rate adjustments of its own,
whose only job is to state how long the household behaves as though it is in a crisis. The
window is the measured peak→trough (`MEASUREMENTS.md` §1), floored at 12 months so it always
spans a period boundary — the strategies are evaluated on period advance, and COVID's
measured 2 months could otherwise be stepped straight over.

| preset | stress window | tags |
|---|---|---|
| `MARKET_CRASH_2008_LITE` | 17 mo | `ECONOMIC_STRESS`, `PANIC_SELL_TRIGGER` |
| `COVID_2020_LITE` | 12 mo (measured 2) | `ECONOMIC_STRESS`, `PANIC_SELL_TRIGGER` |
| `DOTCOM_2000_LITE` | 30 mo | `ECONOMIC_STRESS`, `PANIC_SELL_TRIGGER` |
| `STAGFLATION_1970S_LITE` | 23 mo | `ECONOMIC_STRESS` |
| `LOST_DECADE_2000` | 120 mo | `ECONOMIC_STRESS` |
| `MILD_CORRECTION` | 12 mo (measured 3) | `ECONOMIC_STRESS` |
| `SF_BAY_HOUSING_CRASH`, the three curve shocks | — | none |

Only the **sharp** falls carry `PANIC_SELL_TRIGGER`: panic-selling is an entry reaction, and
nobody panics into year six of a lost decade. `LOST_DECADE_2000`'s 120-month window is the
harshest in the library: read an arm that combines it with `CONTRIBUTION_SUSPENSION` knowing
the household stops contributing for ten years.

### How hard, as opposed to what kind — `regime.severity`

A tag says which KIND of downturn is in play. **`severity` says how hard**, and it is a
separate channel on purpose (design 21 §24). Every regime a shock creates carries the
shock's measured trough depth, and strategies gate on a threshold they own:
`contributionSuspensionMinSeverity` and `cashBucketDrawdownMinSeverity`, both defaulting to
**0.25**. So "only suspend contributions in a medium-to-high stress environment" is a number
you set, not a label the library fixed.

| preset | severity | panics? | suspends? |
|---|---|---|---|
| GFC | 0.51 | yes | yes |
| Lost decade | 0.51 | no | yes |
| Stagflation | 0.43 | no | yes |
| Dot-com | 0.35 | yes | yes |
| COVID | 0.19 | yes | **no** |
| Mild | 0.115 | no | no |

This is why the intensity is **not** a graded tag (`STRESS_LOW/MED/HIGH`): `severity` is the
MC/optimizer knob, and `applySeverity` leaves tags untouched. A preset that declared its own
intensity as a label would still claim to be severe after a sweep had scaled it to a mild
dip. `MILD_CORRECTION` is therefore tagged like the rest, and held back by its depth rather
than by the absence of a tag — sweep it to 0.4 and it correctly starts suspending.

A shock with **no** severity (a custom or curve shock) clears any threshold: an absent number
is missing information, not evidence of mildness.

### Severity

`severity` is the single MC/optimizer knob, and each preset's value is **its measured trough
depth**. It scales the level effect *and* every leg's return drag by the ratio to that
headline, so the shape survives a sweep and only the magnitude moves. For a per-market array
the whole vector scales proportionally, so the US-vs-AU asymmetry survives too. Sweeping
severity deepens a crash; it does not lengthen one.

### `durationMonths` is not the drawdown length

It is the life of the depressed-return regime. Peak→trough and trough→back-to-peak are both
emergent — see design 21 §21, and `CALIBRATION.md` for what each preset produces.

## 2. The knobs, and what each one does to a scenario

| field | units | what reads it | what you see in a run |
|---|---|---|---|
| `levelEffects.equityRevaluation` | multiplier, e.g. `-0.40` = −40 % | `RevalueAssetReducer`, per holding, matched on the holding's own allocation | net worth steps down on the shock date; cost basis unchanged, so unrealized gain falls (and a later sale realizes less CGT) |
| `levelEffects.realEstateRevaluation` | multiplier | same, on `RealProperty.value` | property value steps down. The mortgage does **not**, so equity in the house takes the whole hit |
| `returnAdjustment` | **percentage points per year**, added to the base return | `effectiveGrowthRates` → the equity return path | forward growth is lower for as long as the regime lives. `-0.03` off a 7 % base is 4 % |
| `interestRateAdjustment` | percentage points | `effectiveInterestRates` → savings, bond and Prime keys | cash yields and bond levels move. `PRIME_*` also reprices variable loans |
| `inflationAdjustment` | percentage points | `effectiveInflationRates` → `InflationAdjustReducer` | spending, wages and bracket indexation all move together; the effect on a plan is mostly through *spending* |
| `appreciationAdjustment` | percentage points | `effectiveAppreciationRates` | property appreciates more slowly (or falls) |
| `dividendAdjustment` | **fraction of the payout**, e.g. `-0.40` = pay 40 % less | `DividendScheduledHandler` | dividend income falls, so a plan living off distributions has to sell instead |
| `yieldCurveTwist` | per-tenor spread deltas, **relative to the level move**, anchored at the 5-year point | `YieldCurveReducer` | changes what a *newly bought or rolled* bond earns by tenor; a ladder feels it as it rolls |
| `fxAdjustment` | **additive, in AUD per USD** (base 1.55) | `effectiveExchangeRates.USD_AUD` | cross-border transfers and AUD-denominated spending reprice. `+0.08` on 1.55 is a 5.2 % AUD depreciation |
| `fxVolAdjustment` | **multiplicative on volatility**: `0.5` means ×1.5 | `effectiveFxVol` | only bites when `fxProcessModel` is not `NONE`. With FX pinned, this knob does nothing |

**Policy rates move on `PRIME_*`, never on `SAVINGS_*`.** `PrimeRelinkReducer` *adds* the
Prime delta onto each linked account's savings key, so moving both would cut a linked
account twice. An account authored with a fixed savings rate opted out of the policy link
and correctly does not move (design 21 §18.4).

## 3. The recovery profiles

The curve is a pure function of months-since-shock returning a factor in [0, 1] that
scales the regime's adjustments. `durationMonths` is the total life of the regime, not the
length of the downturn.

| profile | shape | factor path | the episode it is for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **V** | linear fade | 1 → 0 over the whole duration | a shock that starts mending immediately. COVID: NBER dates the contraction at **2 months**, the shortest on record (`NBER-business-cycle-dates.txt`) |
| **U** | flat, then fade | 1 for the first half, then linear to 0 | a shock that *persists* before it mends. The dot-com bust ground for **30 months** peak-to-trough (M §1) — the flat half is the part a bond reserve has to outlast |
| **L** | flat, then snap | 1 throughout, then 0 | a structural shift with no recovery inside the window. The 1970s (a decade of inflation) and the 2000s lost decade |
| **W** | damped cosine | 1 → 0 → 1 → 0 | a double dip. The historical case is real: NBER dates **two** recessions, Jan–Jul 1980 (6 months) and Jul 1981–Nov 1982 (16 months), separated by a 12-month expansion. No shipped preset uses W |
| **V_REBOUND** / **U_REBOUND** | fade, then swing **below zero**, then back | 1 → 0 → −p → 0 | anything with a real recovery. The factor going negative flips the drag into a *tailwind*, which is the only way this framework can model an above-baseline rebound |

The first four are deliberately crude — not fitted curves, but qualitatively different
persistence stories, and the choice between them matters more than the exact
`durationMonths`.

**The rebound pair is what makes a calibration possible at all.** Without it a recovery can
only creep back at baseline: a −50.8 % hole compounding at 7 % needs ~140 months, where the
S&P actually took 65. A preset could match the measured trough or the measured recovery,
never both. Two extra knobs live on the recovery spec — `reboundStart`, the fraction of the
window at which the drag is spent, and `reboundPeak`, how far below zero the factor swings
as a fraction of the original drag.

## 4. The presets

### `MARKET_CRASH_2008_LITE` — Market Crash (GFC-style)

**What it does.** A small level break (−4 %) and then a **−55 pp forward-return drag** that
carries the book down across two year-ends to a −51.9 % trough, before a tailwind returns it
to its prior peak at ~72 months. Dividends cut 22 %, the AUD depreciates and FX gets ~2.9×
choppier, and a separate rates leg cuts policy 5 pp with a bull steepener.

**Why this shape.** The GFC was deep, global and — the part the old preset missed —
**slow**. Only 8 % of the fall happened in the first three months (M §1); the S&P took
17 months to bottom at −50.8 %, with the OECD US broad index at −53.3 % and AU at −49.3 %.
The near-identical US and AU depths are why this preset uses one drag for all four markets,
unlike the dot-com one. Policy: fed funds −4.81 pp, US prime −5.00 pp, AU 3-month −3.50 pp
(M §5), with a measured bull steepener of 1y −1.17 pp / 30y +0.94 pp about the 5-year anchor
(M §6). Dividends: S&P dividends per share fell **22.3 %** (M §3) — the old −40 % was
roughly twice the truth. FX: the AUD fell 36 % in four months and realized volatility ran
**2.87×** baseline (M §7).

**The change that matters most.** This preset used to be a −40 % *instantaneous* break with
a decorative drag: its trough was at month 1. It now takes two years to bottom. For any
study about reserves, buckets or sequencing, that is the difference between a shock the
reserve must bridge and one it cannot.

---

### `COVID_2020_LITE` — Pandemic Crash

**What it does.** A −19.1 % level break carrying the whole drawdown, then a fast tailwind
that regains the prior peak inside two years. Dividends −3 %, mild **dis**inflation, a
1.5 pp policy cut, and an FX spike that reverses.

**Why this shape.** Speed is the defining fact — NBER dates the contraction at **2 months**,
the shortest on record, and **82 %** of the fall was inside three months (M §1). This is the
one preset where an instantaneous break is right, and the drag exists only to set how fast
it comes back. The rebound starts inside the first year, which is the only way to express a
7-month round trip when equity growth is applied annually; the model still floors at 24
months. Rates moved almost in parallel (twist ≤ 0.35 pp, M §6), hence no `yieldCurveTwist`.

**Two corrections from the old version.** Its dividend cut was −30 % against a measured
**−2.3 %** — the largest single calibration error in the library. And it carried +1 pp of
inflation, where CPI YoY actually *fell* to 0.2 % by May 2020; the 2021-22 inflation came
18 months later and this framework cannot start a leg late (design 21 §23), so that belongs
in a second shock you schedule yourself.

**Caveat on the depth.** −19.1 % is a monthly average. The daily peak-to-trough was −33.9 %
and the model has no intra-month resolution — reach for `severity` if that is the case you
want.

---

### `DOTCOM_2000_LITE` — Dot-Com Bust (2000–2002)

**What it does.** Per-market level breaks (−7.4 % US, −2.4 % AU) and **four legs**: a US/
international equity drag over 120 months, a *separate* AU equity leg on a 48-month clock,
a markets leg for dividends and FX, and a rates leg over 84 months.

**Why this shape.** This is the preset whose defining property is **duration, not depth** —
the binding test for a reserve, because a two-year bucket survives a 2008 and is exhausted
by a 2000.

- *Duration.* The S&P took **30 months** to bottom at −43.7 % and did not regain its peak
  for **81 months**; 171 in real terms (M §1). Only 17 % of the fall was front-loaded.
- *Asymmetry, and the reason AU gets its own leg.* The ASX did not peak until **February
  2002 — eighteen months after the S&P** — then fell just 18.5 % over 13 months and was back
  to its peak in 28, before the resources boom took it up 72 % in five years. That is a
  different episode on a different clock, and neither a shared level effect nor a shared
  recovery curve can say it.
- *Bonds rallied.* Fed funds 6.40 % → 0.98 %, prime 9.50 % → 4.00 % (M §5), with a measured
  bull steepener of 1y −1.51 pp / 30y +1.87 pp about the 5-year anchor (M §6).
- *No inflation shock.* CPI drifted 3.8 % → 1.6 % (M §4): mild disinflation.
- *Dividends held up.* S&P dividends per share fell only **6.4 %** (M §3) — the damage was
  in companies that paid none.
- *AU policy did not follow.* The AU 3-month rate fell 0.73 pp while the AU 10-year *rose*
  (M §5). `PRIME_AU: -0.015` against `PRIME_US: -0.045` is that fact, and it is why a
  cross-border plan sees the two countries diverge.

---

### `LOST_DECADE_2000` — Lost Decade (2000–2012)

**What it does.** A −30 % break plus a −5.7 pp US drag held **flat for 120 months (L)**, with
a matching slow rate leg. No fade — the point is the *absence* of a recovery.

**Why this shape.** The sequence-risk case, and the one a reserve actually exists for.
`DOTCOM_2000_LITE` models two bad years inside a sixteen-year plan; a plan that earns its
baseline in fourteen of sixteen years does not need a bond reserve, it needs equity. What
made the era ruinous for a retiree was the decade:

| window | S&P 500 | S&P 500 real | AU broad |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mar 2000 → Mar 2010 | −20.1 % | **−37.2 %** | +50.3 % |
| Aug 2000 → Aug 2010 | −26.8 % | **−42.1 %** | +34.4 % |

(M §2.) Shiller's real total-return index puts the dividend contribution at a measured
+1.8 pp/yr, which still leaves the real total return at **−24.7 % over Mar 2000 → Mar 2010**.
A decade in which US equity delivered less than nothing, and a retiree spending through it
draws down a book that never recovers. The preset is calibrated to that ten-year cumulative
rather than to a trough, and hits −20.3 % against the measured −20.1 %.

**The AU leg is now ~zero drag, on purpose.** Australia did not have a lost decade: the OECD
AU index *gained* 50.3 % over the same window. The old −3 pp was a drag where the record has
none, and it stressed an AU-heavy plan with an episode that never touched it.

⚠️ **An authored stress, not a forecast, and a harsh one.** Run it as an arm beside the
others, never as a constant.

---

### `STAGFLATION_1970S_LITE` — Stagflation

**What it does.** Three legs: an equity leg (−7 % break, −40 pp drag, trough at 24 months),
an inflation leg holding **US +5 pp / AU +4 pp flat for 120 months (L)**, and a rates leg
raising policy 14 pp and bond yields 7 pp for the same decade.

**Why this shape.** The mechanism is inflation, and the damage arrives through *spending* —
which the inflation adjustment drives directly — and through the erosion of a fixed-income
sleeve. US CPI over 1972–1982 averaged **8.3 %** with a peak of 14.6 %, i.e. **+5.76 pp**
over a 2.5 % baseline (M §4). L-shaped because the decade was: three separate waves, no fade
inside any plausible window. The 1979–81 curve moved almost in parallel (twist ≤ 0.9 pp,
M §6), so this is a *level* shock with no twist — and duration is the loss.

**Two fixes from the old version.** It used to carry **no level effect at all**, while the
S&P fell 43.4 % nominal and 59.6 % real across 1973–74 and the ASX fell 54.1 % (M §1) —
modelling the decade as an inflation event only understated it badly. And its duration was
48 months for an episode that ran a decade.

**One number that rests on a series that cannot support it.** The FX leg's `USD_AUD −0.10`
is a *trade-weighted* claim: the AUD was **pegged until 12 December 1983**, so there is no
market USD/AUD history for the 1970s. The major-currencies dollar index fell **10.7 %** over
1973–1980 (M §7b) — right sign, asserted magnitude.

### `MILD_CORRECTION` — Mild Correction

**What it does.** −11.5 % on `EQUITY_US` and −10.9 % on `EQUITY_INTL_EX_AU`, with a fast
rebound that regains the prior peak in 12 months. No FX, no rates, no inflation, **no
dividend cut**, and **no tags** — see §1.

**Why this shape.** The low-severity control arm: the thing that should *not* break a plan,
and therefore the baseline every other preset is read against. Calibrated to 2018 Q4 —
S&P −11.5 % over 3 months, back to peak in 7, Nasdaq −23.6 % (M §1). 100 % of the fall was
inside three months, so it is a pure level break. Dividends **did not move at all** (M §3),
so the −10 % trim the preset used to carry is gone. US-**led** is deliberate: it is the one
preset that leaves `EQUITY_AU` and `EQUITY_INTL_EX_US` alone. `EQUITY_INTL_EX_AU` does move,
because that sleeve is a global-ex-Australia basket — roughly 70 % US by weight — and
leaving it out meant an AU household whose growth sleeve is a global fund felt nothing at
all from the library's control arm. It is priced at the framework's own market-factor
loading for the sleeve (`DEFAULT_EQUITY_BETA` = 0.95), not at a fresh guess.

### `SF_BAY_HOUSING_CRASH` — SF Bay Housing Crash

**What it does.** A −0.6 % break plus a **−31.75 pp appreciation drag** on properties tagged
`market: 'US-SF-BAY'` only, composing to −45.3 % at month 36 and back to the prior peak at
~120. Country-level real estate is untouched, and so is every equity sleeve.

**Why this shape.** It exists to demonstrate that a shock can be *regional* — the level
effect matches a market tag, not a country. The calibration:

| index | peak | trough | depth | grind | back to peak |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Case-Shiller **San Francisco** | Mar 2006 | May 2009 | **−45.3 %** | 38 mo | 116 mo |
| Case-Shiller US national | Jul 2006 | Feb 2012 | −27.4 % | 67 mo | 126 mo |

(M §9.) The regional index fell **1.7×** as far as the national one in a little over half
the time — the reason to model a market rather than a country. The old −35 % was too shallow.

**Housing is the slowest decline in the library.** Only **1 %** of San Francisco's fall
happened in the first three months, against 8 % for the GFC's equity and 82 % for COVID's —
so this preset is almost pure drag, and the level break is a rounding error. That makes it
the sharpest illustration of the §1 composition rule.

For contrast, Australian housing has no comparable episode: BIS's AU residential index fell
4.7 % nominal at its worst, which is why there is deliberately no AU housing preset.

---

### `CURVE_BEAR_FLATTENER`, `CURVE_BULL_STEEPENER`, `CURVE_INVERSION`

**What they do.** Pure term-structure moves — no level effect, no equity, no inflation.
Each applies a per-tenor `yieldCurveTwist` to both countries' curves.

| preset | twist (1y / 5y / 10y / 30y) | profile |
|---|---|---|
| Bear flattener | +1.5 / +0.8 / +0.3 / 0.0 pp | V, 24 mo |
| Bull steepener | −1.5 / −0.8 / −0.2 / 0.0 pp | V, 24 mo |
| Inversion | +2.0 / +0.5 / −0.3 / −0.8 pp (and +1.2 at 2y) | L, 18 mo |

**Why these shapes.** They are the three canonical term-structure regimes, and the
measured episodes back the *pattern* even where the magnitudes are stylized (M §6):

- **Bear flattener** — the 2022 hiking cycle: 1y +4.36 pp against 30y +2.24 pp, i.e. a
  +1.35 pp twist at the front and −0.77 pp at the long end about a +3.01 pp level move.
  The preset states the twist without the level move, so running it alone models the
  *shape* change and not the rate rise; pair it with an `interestRateAdjustment` if you
  want both.
- **Bull steepener** — the dot-com easing: 1y −1.51 pp / 30y +1.87 pp about the 5-year
  anchor. The preset's −1.5 pp front-end twist is that number; its long end (0.0) is
  materially milder than the +1.87 pp that actually happened.
- **Inversion** — 2000 (+0.96 pp at 1y, −0.21 pp at 30y) and 2006 (+2.40 pp / −1.46 pp).
  The preset sits close to the 2006 episode, which is the more pronounced of the two.
  L/18 months matches how long these inversions persisted before resolving.

These are the presets to reach for when the question is about a **bond ladder's roll**
rather than about a crash: a twist changes what newly bought and rolled rungs earn, and a
short ladder feels it far faster than a long one.

## 5. What the framework cannot express

Every preset is now calibrated to a measured episode and `CALIBRATION.md` prints the fit.
What remains are **limits of the framework**, not of the numbers — and they bias results in
particular directions, so they are worth knowing before quoting one.

### Hard limits

| limit | what it costs you |
|---|---|
| **Equity growth is applied once a year.** | A decline is expressible only in whole years, so the GFC preset bottoms at 24 months against a measured 17, and a fast recovery has a 12-month floor (COVID: 24 modelled vs 7 measured). Cash and fixed income *are* applied monthly, so one preset has two different time resolutions. |
| **A leg cannot start late.** Every leg begins at the shock date. | COVID's real inflation arrived ~18 months after the crash. The preset models the disinflation that came *with* it; the inflation that followed has to be a second shock you schedule yourself. |
| **A shock cannot ramp up.** The factor starts at full strength. | The 1970s built to a peak over ~24 months and had a second wave at ~87. The preset flattens both into one sustained level at the decade's average excess. |
| **No equity volatility knob.** A return is a rate the shock shifts, not a distribution whose shape it changes. | VIX averaged 40 through the GFC against 13.7 in 2004–06 (M §8) and none of that variance is expressible. A shock changes the mean, never the spread. |
| **No correlation regime.** | "Everything correlates to 1 in a crash" cannot be modelled, and neither can the dot-com case where bonds rallied *because* equity fell. Presets approximate it by moving both keys by hand — which means the diversification benefit in a result is authored, not emergent. |
| **Rebound shape is per leg, not per market.** | `DOTCOM_2000_LITE` needs a separate `equity-au` leg purely because the ASX recovered on a different clock. It works, but it does not scale past a handful of markets. |
| **Depths are monthly averages.** | The model has no intra-month resolution. COVID's daily peak-to-trough was −33.9 % against the −19.1 % the preset uses. |

### Numbers with no source on disk

Three figures in the library are **not measured**, and are flagged in place so nobody
"verifies" them against the wrong series:

- **Every AU and INTERNATIONAL `dividendAdjustment` is the US figure.** There is no free scripted AU dividend
  series — the RBA path that would carry it 404s (see `SOURCES.md`). The COVID case is the
  one most likely wrong in a known direction: APRA's 2020 capital guidance cut bank payouts
  hard, and banks are a far larger share of the ASX than of the S&P. The two international
  sleeves carry the US figure for the same reason. `EQUITY_INTL_EX_AU` is ~70 % US by
  construction so it is close to right; `EQUITY_INTL_EX_US` is asserted. Naming only US and
  AU, as the library used to, was not the cheaper claim — `effectiveDividendAdjustments` is
  keyed by the *holding's* rate key, so an unnamed sleeve took the price hit and went on
  paying its full yield straight through the crash.
- **The 1970s FX leg.** The AUD floated on **12 December 1983**; every earlier USD/AUD
  observation is an administered rate. The claim is trade-weighted (major-currencies dollar
  −10.7 % over 1973–80, M §7b) — sign supported, magnitude asserted.
- **`fxVolAdjustment` only bites when `fxProcessModel` is not `NONE`.** With FX pinned it
  does nothing at all, silently.

### The thing most likely to mislead

Each preset is calibrated to **one historical episode**, which is what makes it checkable —
and also makes it specific in a way a forecast is not. Composing two presets does not
produce "a worse crash" so much as the arithmetic sum of two particular decades. The honest
use of this library is **comparing strategies across a shared set of shock arms**, not
quoting an absolute outcome from any single one.

---

## 6. How to check a number

```bash
docs/economic-shocks/fetch-sources.sh                       # refresh data/
node scripts/probes/measure-shock-history.mjs --write       # what HAPPENED  → MEASUREMENTS.md
node scripts/probes/shock-path-engine.mjs     --write       # what the presets DO → CALIBRATION.md
node scripts/probes/shock-path-engine.mjs --start-sensitivity
```

`shock-path-engine.mjs` runs every preset through the real simulator and prints depth,
trough month, back-to-peak and the ten-year cumulative beside the measured episode. It is
the file to look at after changing any preset — composing a path on paper gives the wrong
answer, because it misses that equity growth is applied annually.

The probe reads only the CSVs in `data/`. If you want a window or an episode that is not
in `MEASUREMENTS.md`, add it to the `EPISODES` / `*_WINDOWS` tables at the top of the
sections in the script — that is what those tables are for.

`--start-sensitivity` moves the same shock around the calendar. Because the recovery factor
is only sampled at year-end, a composed preset's depth depends on where its curve sits on
31 December: `DOTCOM_2000_LITE` spans 5.2 points between a January and an October start,
while a pure level break like `MILD_CORRECTION` spans none.
